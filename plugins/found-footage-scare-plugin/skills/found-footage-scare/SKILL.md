# Found-footage scare videos

A jumpscare is a **contrast machine**. The hit itself is easy — a loud noise and a scary picture. Everything that makes it work happens in the twenty seconds beforehand, and almost every failed scare fails because something in that build-up warned the viewer.

Hold that idea while making every decision below. When in doubt, ask: *does this tell the viewer something is coming?* If yes, cut it.

## The arc

Six beats. Durations are a starting point, not a rule.

| beat | length | what it does |
|---|---|---|
| **lull** | 8–12s | Empty plate. Nothing happens. Deliberately boring. |
| **reveal** | 6–10s | Slow crossfade bringing something subtle into frame. |
| **creep** | 3s | Hold. Let them squint and doubt themselves. |
| **dropout** | 2–4 frames | Black. The recorder stutters. |
| **hit** | 2–3s | The scare, hard cut, no fade. |
| **aftermath** | 4–6s | Back to the empty plate. *Was that real?* |

**The lull must actually be boring.** New users shorten it because it feels dead. That deadness is the product — it's what resets the viewer's attention and their sense of how loud the video is. Five seconds is not enough; ten works.

**The aftermath is the best beat and the one people skip.** Returning to the empty plate, quieter than the lull was, is what turns a cheap shock into something that sits with people. Don't end on the scare.

## What telegraphs (and therefore what to avoid)

A viewer who braces does not jump. These all brace them:

- **Glitching, shaking or distorting before the hit.** This is the big one, and it's counter-intuitive because instability *feels* like horror. It isn't — it's a warning. Put the distortion **on the hit itself**, where it reads as the camera failing to cope. Before the hit, keep the frame still.
- **Music that swells.** Any rising element scores the build-up as a build-up. Leave the lull unscored.
- **A push-in before the reveal.** Camera movement implies an operator who knows something.
- **Getting shorter.** Cuts that accelerate signal a climax.

If someone asks for a shake or glitch before the hit, build it but say plainly why it weakens the scare and make it a variable they can set to zero. They may want it for a non-scare reason.

## What sells "this is a real recording"

- **Constant grain over everything.** A perfectly frozen still announces that nothing will happen — no sensor produces a motionless frame. A light temporal noise pass (`noise=alls=7:allf=t+u`) over the whole finished sequence keeps every shot alive and ambiguous. This matters more than any other realism trick.

  Be aware this fights the bitrate matching below: at a camera-like ~1 Mbps, x264 quantises light grain to nothing on static shots and then restores it abruptly, which reads as a jump. Add `-tune grain` when encoding at low bitrates, and check it with `scripts/verify_sequence.py` — judge it by the size of the restore spike, not by how many frames went still. Measured at 1 Mbps 720p: default leaves a 9.44 YDIF pop, `-tune grain` reduces it to 2.03.
- **A few frames of black before the hit.** Reads as dropped frames, and it masks the cut so the scare arrives with no visible edge to anticipate.
- **An on-screen timestamp that skips across the dropout.** If the clock loses five seconds during a tenth of a second of black, it tells the viewer something had time to cross the garden. Genuinely unsettling, and impossible to fake with a real recording. The OSD should stay lit *through* the black, because the timestamp is generated by the camera chip, not the sensor.
- **Provenance that agrees with itself.** See "Passing as a camera export" below.

## Workflow

### 1. Read the source images before planning

Look at them. The pacing depends on how subtle the reveal is and what the payoff looks like. A reveal that's easy to miss wants a longer creep; a reveal that's obvious wants a shorter one. You cannot make this judgement from filenames.

Check dimensions early — **odd width or height breaks H.264 `yuv420p`**. Stills that are, say, 1672×941 must be scaled or cropped to even numbers. See `references/ffmpeg-gotchas.md`.

### 2. Build the silent sequence

`scripts/make_sequence.sh` is a working, parameterised implementation of the arc — timings, OSD, clock skip and grain all as variables at the top. Copy it into the user's project and edit the variables rather than rebuilding the filter graph from scratch.

Structure: each beat becomes a looped image input; the reveal is an `xfade`; everything else is `concat`; grain and the timestamp overlay go on last, after concatenation, so they're uniform across the whole clip.

Scale every beat through the *same* `scale`/`crop` pair even when only one of them needs the slack, or you get a visible pop where the geometry changes between shots.

### 3. Choose and prepare the audio bed

Do not pick a bed by listening alone, and do not assume several clips from the same camera are interchangeable. Measure them. `scripts/analyze_audio.py` reports loudness, band energy, transient clusters and the quietest usable window.

Two things worth checking that the loudness figures won't show you: **mains hum** (a 40 Hz highpass sits below 50 Hz and leaves it untouched — probe 48/50/52 against 60/80 and notch if it stands proud), and, if you have to loop, whether the head and tail actually match in level. Both are in `references/audio-forensics.md`.

**LRA is the tell for a bed.** Integrated loudness tells you how loud a clip is; loudness range tells you whether anything *happens* in it. A usable bed measures around 1–2 LU across its length. A clip measuring 20+ LU has events in it, however quiet it sounds.

Before measuring anything, **extract a frame and read the camera's burned-in clock** — most CCTV carries one, and it is the only trustworthy record of when a clip was shot. Filename epochs and `creation_time` are export/mux times: on one real set the filename epoch was 6 hours off and ordered two clips backwards.

Watch for day-vs-night mismatches when the plate is night footage. Daytime ambience under an IR night image is an instant tell, and the discriminator is **saturation, not brightness** — IR illuminators make night footage as bright as day, but night mode is exactly monochrome. `signalstats` `SATAVG` reads 0.00 for IR and clearly non-zero for daylight.

### 4. Design the sting

Covered in depth in `references/sting-design.md`. The headlines:

- **Align onsets, not peaks.** Files have differing amounts of leading silence; measure it and compensate, or your layers smear.
- **Audio leads picture by about two frames.** ~66ms at 30fps. Landing them on the same frame reads as *late*.
- **When two layers collide spectrally, separate them in time, not frequency.** Two screams that both peak in 1–4 kHz cannot be EQ'd apart. Make one a short impact and let the other carry the sustain.
- **Synthesise the sub.** Most scream samples have no energy below 200 Hz. A sine sweeping ~55→25 Hz with a fast attack and exponential decay supplies the body, and it's the difference between a scare you hear and one you feel.

### 5. Mix with real headroom

`scripts/mix_sting.sh` implements the mix. Two things that will otherwise cost an hour:

- `amix` normalises by default — pass `normalize=0` or the bed ducks 6 dB the moment the sting lands.
- `alimiter` has **auto-level enabled by default**, so lowering `limit` doesn't lower the output; it renormalises back up to the new ceiling. Pass `level=disabled`. It also caps *sample* peaks while true (intersample) peaks run ~1.5 dB higher, so leave real headroom rather than tightening the ceiling.
- Trim the bus a few dB *before* the limiter. Summed layers can hit +7 dBFS, and a limiter doing 8 dB of reduction flattens the transient the sting exists to deliver — reproducing the crushed sound of the sample you were trying to fix.
- `aformat=channel_layouts=stereo` silently costs mono sources 3.01 dB. Use `pan=stereo|c0=c0|c1=c0` for those, and leave stereo sources alone.

Calibration from a mix that worked:

```
lull              -49 dB        integrated  -15.4 LUFS
impact            -12 dB        LRA          22.6 LU
sustain           -17 to -24    true peak    -1.9 dBFS
tail over empty   -42 dB
aftermath         -50 dB        ← quieter than the lull
```

A ~37 dB jump from lull to impact is the mechanism. The quiet bed makes people turn the volume up; that's what makes the hit loud. Anything that normalises the lull upward destroys the effect.

These are what `scripts/mix_sting.sh` produces at its shipped defaults, measured. Absolute loudness matters far less than the size of that jump — a mix that lands 3 dB quieter overall but keeps the gap is the better one.

### 6. Verify by measuring, not by watching

You cannot hear the output, so check it numerically — and say plainly which judgements you can't make. Confirm the audio onset lands the intended number of frames ahead of the cut, that the level arc matches the shape above, and that true peak is under −1 dBFS. Extract frames at each beat boundary into a contact sheet to confirm the visual sequence.

What you genuinely cannot assess: whether two voices sound like the same person in the same space, and whether a scare actually lands. Say so rather than implying otherwise.

## Passing as a camera export

Only relevant if the clip is meant to look like genuine footage — a prank, or an ARG. If so, everything must agree, and the mismatches are usually mechanical:

- **File size is the giveaway.** A 68 MB clip for 29 seconds is not something a security camera produces. Match the source's bits-per-second.
- **Match the real exports' container**: resolution, frame rate, `major_brand`, and any device tags like `com.android.version`.
- **Strip the encoder tag.** `-fflags +bitexact -flags:v +bitexact -flags:a +bitexact` removes ffmpeg's `Lavf…` signature.
- **Every date must agree**: filename, embedded `creation_time`, and the burned-in OSD clock. `creation_time` is UTC and displays converted to local, so derive it rather than reasoning about the offset — see `references/ffmpeg-gotchas.md`. Getting this wrong by an hour is the easiest way to undo all the other work.
- **Don't date it in the future.** A file stamped 31 October when today is 12 September defeats the whole exercise.

One deliberate exception is worth keeping: **the audio can exceed what the source device could record.** A camera doing 8 kHz mono gives you a bed capped at 4 kHz; when the sting arrives carrying energy above that, it audibly isn't coming from the camera. That asymmetry is an asset — engineer it rather than sanding it off. Nobody inspects a sample rate before pressing play.

Build files that are honestly what they claim to be in *substance* — a Halloween video that looks like CCTV is a costume, not a forgery. Don't extend this technique to fabricating records of events, impersonating a real organisation's footage, or anything presented as genuine evidence.

## Incidental recordings of real people

Security-camera audio picks up passers-by. If analysis surfaces a voice or other identifiable content in the source, flag it and leave it out of the deliverable unless the user says otherwise — and don't propose using it as "atmosphere". Mention which working files still contain it so the user can decide what to keep. Say it once, plainly, and move on; it's a practical note, not a lecture.
